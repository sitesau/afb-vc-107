What changes when the purchase is your first

A first-home file is usually simpler than a complex commercial deal, but it has less room for error. The target guide says a broker sits between you and lenders, matching you to a suitable loan, preparing the application and managing lender questions until settlement. In practice, that means your broker should turn a vague goal, such as buying near Glenelg or Norwood, into a borrowing position before auction or private-treaty deadlines.

The parent page also separates brokers from matching services. A matching service vets a network and pairs you with a specialist; the licensed broker still gives credit assistance. Keep that distinction clear so you know who is advising, who is administering, and who is accountable for the recommendation.

Five checks before you book an appointment

  1. Licence: confirm the adviser holds or works under an Australian Credit Licence, with ASIC registers used as the check.
  2. Panel breadth: thirty lenders is useful; around fifty gives more room when your deposit, employment or property type is tight.
  3. First-home specialty: ask for recent first-buyer files and how they handled valuation gaps.
  4. Local reach: choose someone who knows greater Adelaide, from CBD apartments to Hills and outer-suburb stock.
  5. Communication: you should get plain answers, a document list and a realistic timeline, not jargon.

Two concepts deserve plain treatment before you compare options. Loan-to-value ratio is the loan size compared with the property value, and a higher ratio can narrow lender choice. Lenders mortgage insurance may apply where the deposit is smaller, changing both cost and which lenders compete for the file. The supplied texts do not quote current premiums or grant rules, so treat any precise number as a prompt to verify with the broker and ASIC Moneysmart rather than a promise.

For first buyers, the information gain is sequencing: clarify deposit position, understand whether insurance or stricter credit criteria apply, then shortlist lenders. That prevents a common error where a buyer shops for a rate before knowing whether the lender will accept the property, the income evidence or the settlement timing.

How the process should feel from first call to keys

The target guide lists a standard journey: discovery, comparison, application, approval and settlement. For a first home, the broker should use discovery to test affordability under today's rates, then comparison to show why one lender suits the property and your profile. Application is where tidy paperwork matters; approval may include valuation and conditional stages; settlement is the release of funds and the start of repayments.

Keep pressure low by asking for a written timeline after discovery. If the file is clean, four to six weeks is a reasonable planning frame in the parent text, while self-employed or less standard files can take longer. When a lender is slow, the value of an experienced broker is anticipating questions before they stall approval.

Common questions

Is a broker usually free for a first-home buyer? For the vast majority of home and consumer loans in the parent guide, the broker is paid by the lender once the loan settles. Some commercial or complex deals may carry a fee, so ask for written disclosure before you commit.

How do I avoid choosing a sales channel instead of a broker? Use the five checks: licence, panel size, specialty, local reach and communication. The parent guide also notes that free does not automatically mean unbiased, so ask how many lenders are on the panel.

What if my property or income is unusual? Say so early. Self-employment, tight deposits, character homes and auction timing can change lender appetite. A specialist should explain which lenders are likely to be flexible and what documents they need first.
